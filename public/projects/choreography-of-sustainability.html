Listen to the system

In 2023, the world's population crossed 8 billion people, a number expected to rise dramatically by the end of this century. Humanity has spread across nearly every corner of the planet while simultaneously dreaming beyond it.

Most of us live in urban cities, juggling with the highs & lows of our economy. Beneath these systems lie deeply rooted social hierarchies shaped by class, caste, labour, and access, where most men drink coffee out of a filter—and the others who make filters, remaining invisible within the systems that sustain them.

These societies rely on agriculture and beverages to survive. Yet in the larger autobiography of humanity these systems took form very recently, just within a 1 mil years - emerging only within a small fraction of our existence on Earth.

Chapter 01

The Civilisation Soup

"I built my world."

The civilisation soup came into being because of an age old secret ingredient, favourable climatic conditions. Humanity's quest to know and learn gave rise to creative ways of settling and living. Over time, people began forming social structures and systems of governance that allowed communities to live and let live. Religions and scriptures emerged as shared frameworks of language, belief, and meaning.

At the centre of it all was agriculture. Then, as now, it remained an essential ingredient. Without it, every other ingredient in civilisation would begin to fall apart.

This eventually led to the settlement of ideologies and rituals, rooted in collective ways of living on and with the land. Shared beliefs began shaping communities, giving people a common language for identity, purpose, and belonging.

Over time, traces of elitism emerged through the rise of citadels, temples, and centres of power. From these spaces grew the aromas of profession, hierarchy, and organised religion, each defining new roles within society.

Agriculture evolved into a deeply specialised sector, opening doors to newer professions, trades, and interests. Much like a soup absorbing new ingredients over time, civilisation became richer, denser, and more layered with every passing era.

Eventually, these systems became the recipe that shaped the boundaries of family, labour, class, and aspiration, often restricting human thought to the scale of individual lifespans rather than the continuity of generations.

"People then and now are concerned with matters that affect only their family, over a short period of time."

— Limits to Growth

We operate within the boundaries of our own small systems so deeply that it becomes difficult to look beyond the fences of our immediate lives. Our decisions are often shaped by what affects us today, this week, or within the span of our own lifetime. Yet beyond these personal boundaries lies a much larger system that desperately needs our attention.

The internet, energy grids, agriculture, water networks, transportation, climate, and biodiversity are not isolated entities. They are deeply interconnected systems that stretch across nations and generations. Their effects move far beyond the scale at which most of us are conditioned to think. As illustrated in the graph, human attention tends to shrink as space and time expand. We care deeply about family, neighbourhoods, and immediate communities in the short term, but far less about systems that operate across continents or across future generations. The farther a problem moves from our personal horizon, the easier it becomes to ignore.

This narrowing of perspective has shaped the way modern civilisation functions. We optimise for quarterly growth over ecological stability, convenience over resilience, and individual gain over collective continuity.

Complexity arrived with humans.

As populations grew, so did our appetite for resources. What once began as adaptation slowly transformed into control. We moved from living with nature to taming it, shaping landscapes, rivers, forests, and eventually even the silent rhythms of the earth to fit human ambition.

Curiosity was once a tool for survival. It taught us how to read seasons, understand soil, preserve food, and coexist with uncertainty. But over time, the desire to learn evolved into the desire to extract, dominate, and optimise endlessly.

The recipe of civilisation began evolving.
Fear of scarcity led to hoarding. Hoarding created systems of value and trade. Resources became symbols of power, while rituals emerged to preserve meaning and continuity around the very earth that sustained us. At the same time, abundance encouraged extraction, and extraction fuelled exploitation in cycles that slowly became mindless.

Humanity did not arrive at these vulgar systems overnight. We assembled them ingredient by ingredient, belief by belief, generation by generation.

Chapter 02

Anthropocene epoch

"I want my world to be bigger."

So we continued to nurture, nourish, and expand ourselves and our young. The Agricultural Revolution enabled permanent settlement, and with settlement came the onset of technologies, tools, and systems that transformed human civilisation forever.

Curious and learning humans began building a world designed to make living easier. By the 1700s, the Age of Reason had arrived, bringing rigorous scientific, political, and philosophical discourse. Humanity was beginning to understand the laws of the natural world, the mechanics of matter, and even the movements of planets beyond Earth. With this came invention.

Synthetic materials emerged. Parkesine, one of the earliest forms of plastic, was created. Engines powered factories. Ships crossed oceans faster than ever before. Vehicles reshaped mobility and trade. Human imagination was no longer limited to survival. It became a force of production.

A world of trade, a world of making is growing. I can think and so I shall, I can make and so I shall. It all only made our lives much much easier. the lives of a few easier at least. Existing systems of hierarchy and power only deepened through industrial expansion. Humanity entered a new state of wanting more. Curiosity was no longer about sustained survival, it had also become about controlling it, extracting from it.

Learning and knowledge makes you certain right? Definitions gave us a sense of certainty and so did numbers. We began defining the world around us through categories, measurements, and systems. Soon, we defined something even larger for ourselves: progress. It became the ambitions of every growing nation. To move forward meant to move upward.

We constantly looked ahead, rarely pausing to look around us or behind us at the systems we were leaving altered in the process. This urge for certainty was also linked to being able to measure. It only gave us a sense of certainty. However we established systems based on measurement and counting which furthered the notion of control and authority. It amplified and rooted itself in systems even today.

The Industrial Revolution transformed human life in unprecedented ways. It enabled mass production, faster transportation, modern medicine, communication systems, public infrastructure, and access to goods that were once considered luxuries.

But alongside these advancements came acceleration, leading to the emergence of the capitalist economy. Consumption was key to keep the economy running and hence a scarcity was created in the society by the means of planned and perceived obsolescence.

Systems designed for growth often overlooked the limits of the ecosystems supporting them. We developed a mindset where another's consumption is not my own consumption. This disparity led to us foster a lack of sense of finiteness. The world has enough for what i need which is inexorbitantly less.

"The rich got richer & the poor got children."

— Limits to Growth

By the late 1900s, traces of caution began surfacing across the world. Economists, ecologists, scientists, and systems thinkers started asking an uncomfortable question: can infinite growth truly exist on a finite planet?

Books like Limits to Growth challenged the dominant imagination of progress. They pointed toward a future where rising populations, accelerating consumption, and relentless extraction could eventually collide with the Earth's ecological limits. The phrase, "The rich got richer and the poor got children," reflected a growing awareness of how unevenly growth, opportunity, and resources were being distributed across societies.

At around the same time in the 1970s, humanity experienced a profound shift in perspective. For the first time, we saw Earth from afar. The image of Earth rising quietly above the moon's horizon became more than a photograph. It became a mirror. A fragile blue planet suspended in darkness, without visible borders, hierarchies, economies, or nations. This image, as Carl Sagan called it "The pale blue dot", changed human consciousness.

It reminded us that all our systems, conflicts, ambitions, industries, and civilisations existed within a single interconnected sphere. The planet was no longer an abstract backdrop to human progress. It was home. Finite, delicate, and deeply shared. And within that realisation emerged a quiet but powerful sense of hope.

If human systems were built by human imagination, they could also be reimagined. If extraction and excess were learned behaviours, then balance and regeneration could be learned too. The same curiosity that once drove extraction could now drive restoration.

The Anthropocene became both a warning and an awakening. It revealed the immense scale of human influence on Earth, but also our capacity for reflection, adaptation, and change.

Chapter 03

Crisis of Perception

One idea that captured this growing concern was the "Tragedy of the Commons," proposed by Garrett Hardin in 1968. The theory described a simple but dangerous pattern. When shared resources are treated as infinite and freely available, individual pursuit of growth eventually leads to collective depletion. What benefits one actor in the short term may slowly damage the larger system everyone depends upon.

During this period, sustainability began emerging as a critical framework for understanding the relationship between economy, society, and environment. It was no longer possible to think of economic growth in isolation from ecological health or social wellbeing. This gave rise to the idea of different forms of capital.

Human, or produced, capital referred to infrastructure, labour, technology, institutions, and knowledge systems created by people.

Natural capital referred to environmental assets such as forests, soil, water, fossil fuels, biodiversity, and ecosystem functions that sustain life itself.

From these ideas emerged two contrasting perspectives. Strong sustainability argued that human capital and natural capital are complementary but not interchangeable. A forest could not simply be replaced by a machine. Clean rivers, stable climates, fertile soil, and biodiversity carried functions that industrial systems alone could never fully reproduce.

Weak sustainability, however, proposed that natural resources depleted by one generation could eventually be substituted through human innovation, infrastructure, and technological advancement. In this view, man made capital could compensate for environmental loss. The supporting visual reflects this distinction clearly.

These debates fundamentally shifted the way industries, governments, economists, and capitalists began viewing environmental degradation. For the first time at a global scale, humanity began confronting a difficult realisation: the crisis unfolding around us was not only a crisis of resources. It was also a crisis of perception.

The late 1900s became one of the most critical periods in modern history. Environmental crises, technological advancement, political shifts, and scientific discoveries were all unfolding simultaneously at a global scale.

The emergence of systems thinking helped humanity better understand complexity and interdependence. People who stepped beyond their mental fences began building world models that revealed how deeply connected human economies, societies, and natural ecosystems truly were.

In 1962, Rachel Carson published Silent Spring, exposing the long term ecological effects of pesticide use on farmlands and ecosystems. A decade later, Donella Meadows and her team published The Limits to Growth, using computer modelling to demonstrate how exponential economic and population growth could collide with finite planetary resources.

For the first time, finiteness was no longer philosophical. It became measurable, visible, and difficult to ignore.

As the realities of ecological limits became clearer, new philosophies began emerging from outside the rigid frameworks of industrial progress and economic growth. These ideas attempted to soften the frozen ideologies that had long separated humans from nature.

  • In 1972, Arne Næss introduced the philosophy of Deep Ecology, encouraging humanity to see itself not as masters of nature, but as participants within it. As he wrote: "The smaller we come to feel ourselves compared to the mountain, the nearer we come to participating in its greatness."
  • A few years later, in 1975, Peter Berg and Raymond F. Dasmann developed the idea of Bioregionalism. It imagined a future where economic and social systems could become more localised, democratic, place based, and self sustaining by aligning closely with natural ecosystems and regional identities.

These emerging ideas slowly began reshaping public consciousness. They challenged the assumption that progress only meant expansion, centralisation, and endless growth.

Among the many concepts that emerged during this period, one idea managed to move through global institutions and into mainstream governance.

"Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs."

— Our Common Future, 1987

For perhaps the first time in mainstream policy and governance, the future was formally included within the definition of progress itself.

Chapter 04

The Invisible Hand

Let's pause once to look at this artefact of the anthropocene.
A plastiglomerate. A new kind of stone formed not by geology alone, but by humans. Melted plastic fused with sand, coral, shells, wood, and volcanic rock through heat and waste. Neither fully natural nor fully synthetic. A fossil of human behaviour. Some scientists now consider plastiglomerates potential markers of the Anthropocene epoch, physical evidence that human activity has altered the geological fabric of the planet itself.

And so, as ideas of collective responsibility began surfacing across the twentieth century, the world slowly recognised the need for a larger coordinating force.

Institutions like the United Nations began taking a central role in shaping global conversations around peace, development, environment, and human wellbeing. Emerging from the aftermath of world wars and accelerating globalisation, these bodies were formed with the intention of fostering cooperation across nations that were deeply interconnected, yet unevenly developed. The intent was ambitious. To create frameworks where humanity could think collectively about shared futures instead of isolated national interests alone. With rising number of protests, philosophies, sustainability slowly evolved from a philosophical concern into an international developmental agenda.

The supporting visual captures where we stand today. On one side are the planetary boundaries, the ecological thresholds humanity must remain within to sustain life safely on Earth. On the other are the Sustainable Development Goals, representing humanity's aspirations for equity, health, education, prosperity, and wellbeing.

Together, they reveal a profound truth: human progress can no longer exist separately from planetary health.

Though vision was collective, implementation became uneven.

  • The divide between developed and developing nations continued shaping climate negotiations, resource access, and accountability. Countries that industrialised early had already accumulated wealth, infrastructure, and ecological debt, while developing nations were expected to balance growth, poverty reduction, and sustainability simultaneously. This often resulted in unreasonable targets, misplaced funding priorities, and unequal responsibilities.
  • Even within the Sustainable Development Goals, tensions began surfacing between competing objectives. Economic growth could sometimes conflict with ecological restoration. Industrial expansion could undermine biodiversity. For example, a country may expand coal powered energy to provide electricity, jobs, and industrial growth for millions of people. Economically, this supports SDG 8. Environmentally, however, it can directly conflict with SDG 13 and even SDG 15 (Life on Land).
  • Success increasingly depended on accountability, participation, local adaptation, and long term thinking rather than short political or economic cycles.

The flaws of the hand were not proof that collective effort had failed. They were proof that humanity was still learning how to govern interconnected systems at a planetary scale.

Chapter 05

'Progress'

As sustainability conversations expanded beyond policy rooms and institutions, it became important to understand something more intimate: How do people themselves perceive nature?

To answer this, we began mapping encounters across layers of stakeholders and scales of interaction, from isolated individuals to communities, corporations, governance systems, and national policies. These were not merely environmental relationships. They were behavioural and societal links.

Secondary research helped reveal how influence travels across systems despite physical or social distance. National environmental acts, sustainability policies, climate frameworks, media narratives, educational systems, and corporate behaviour all quietly shape public perception. Even when people feel distant from governance structures, policies still influence the products they consume, the spaces they inhabit, and the environmental choices available to them.

Mapping these invisible connections also helped uncover barriers, gaps in awareness, and the routes through which influence spreads. But secondary research alone could not explain lived behaviour. Primary research became essential to understand the mindsets of people directly. The intention was not simply to measure environmental awareness, but to study how individuals emotionally and behaviourally relate to nature itself.

The questions became:

  • How do people perceive nature?
  • What forms of interaction do they have with it?
  • What priorities are considered necessary in everyday life?
  • How do those priorities influence environmental perception and behaviour?

What emerged was a spectrum of perception.
On one end, nature was viewed primarily as an aesthetic experience, something beautiful to see, visit, photograph, or momentarily escape into. On the other end existed a deeper willingness to nurture, where individuals consciously took actions to protect, restore, or care for ecological systems.

Between these two ends lay a significant gap. Many people expressed concern for the environment, yet there was often limited understanding of how direct and indirect actions contribute to ecological degradation or restoration. Sustainability was emotionally acknowledged, but not always behaviourally integrated.

The insights revealed another recurring pattern.
Environmental concern is often driven first by the self, through physical wellbeing, emotional comfort, safety, or personal growth. Beyond the self comes concern for immediate family and close communities. Only after these layers do larger ecological or planetary systems begin entering public consciousness. This reflects the same behavioural boundaries discussed earlier in civilisation's story. Human attention naturally contracts toward immediate worlds.

External entities also play a powerful role in shaping perception. Governments, educational institutions, corporations, laws, social norms, and media ecosystems influence how people understand their relationship with nature. In environments where interaction with ecology is limited, nature often remains an external or aesthetic entity rather than an actively nurtured system.

Yet familiarity changes perception.
The closer people feel to natural systems through farming, gardening, shared public spaces, local biodiversity, childhood experiences, or community interaction, the stronger the willingness to protect and participate becomes.

Perhaps sustainability was never only a technological or policy challenge.
Perhaps it was always, at its core, a challenge of perception.

Until now, the story has largely been about understanding the systems we built, the perceptions we inherited, and the ecological consequences that emerged from them. But awareness alone does not create transformation.

If sustainability is ultimately a behavioural and systemic challenge, then the leverage point lies not only in policies or technologies, but in mindsets, relationships, and participation.

A shift in mindset may be the most effective leverage point we possess.

The research revealed that people move from passive appreciation of nature toward active stewardship only when sustainability becomes contextual, visible, and emotionally proximate. Abstract global goals rarely translate into action unless individuals can locate themselves within the system they are being asked to protect. This became the foundation of our approach. the intention was to contextualise global frameworks like the Sustainable Development Goals and planetary boundaries within regional and lived realities.

The question was no longer simply, "How do we make people aware?" but rather: How might we help people recognise the ecological boundaries and systems they are already a part of?

This approach draws from the ideas of Deep Ecology and Bioregionalism discussed earlier. It proposes that meaningful environmental action emerges when people feel connected to place, community, and local ecosystems rather than disconnected global abstractions.

Within the user cohorts we studied, one group stood out significantly: urban youth(8).

Many young people today already possess awareness about environmental crises and natural systems. They understand climate anxiety, pollution, biodiversity loss, and sustainability discourse. Yet action often remains limited because responsibility feels externalised. Someone else, governments, corporations, institutions, is expected to act first. This creates a condition of high knowledge but low action.

At the same time, younger children(6) displayed something equally important: willingness. Their environmental concern was often more instinctive, participatory, and emotionally driven. They showed curiosity, eagerness to act, and a desire to influence others around them. This revealed an important opportunity.

Perhaps sustainability communication should not only focus on increasing awareness, but on converting awareness into agency. The goal becomes enabling small chains of participation that can ripple outward into communities, conversations, and everyday behaviour.

Chapter 06

Act

To understand how this might unfold in reality, we grounded the study within the context of Bengaluru.

Bangalore is a city of contradictions. It is shaped by rapid urbanisation, technological growth, migration, innovation, and aspiration. At the same time, it faces disappearing lakes, groundwater stress, waste management challenges, traffic congestion, heat islands, and shrinking ecological memory.

But more importantly, Bangalore is a city of movement. Thousands of young people migrate here every year from different towns, cities, and cultural backgrounds in search of education, employment, opportunity, and identity. In many ways, the city has become a shared urban habitat for transient aspirations. Yet in this movement, something often gets lost.

Many urban youth living in Bangalore remain disconnected from the ecological and cultural sensibilities of the land itself. There is little understanding of the region's indigenous knowledge systems, water networks, native biodiversity, seasonal rhythms, agricultural histories, or the ways communities once lived with the landscape here.

The city becomes a place to occupy, consume, and move through, rather than a system to understand, respect, or participate within. Most urban residents encounter nature only in fragmented forms, roadside trees, gated parks, ornamental landscapes, or occasional escapes outside the city. Ecological systems remain physically close, yet psychologically distant. And still, Bangalore carries traces of another possibility.

The city also holds active communities that are already working toward ecological restoration and collective stewardship. Lake rejuvenation groups, climate collectives, school ecosystems, terrace gardeners, citizen movements, local institutions, and neighbourhood initiatives reveal that participation already exists in scattered but powerful forms.

What is often missing is not concern, but connection. Not awareness, but pathways for sustained engagement with the land and its stories.

Instead of designing sustainability only at the scale of abstract global systems, we began asking:

  1. How might urban youth in Bangalore recognise the ecological systems surrounding them as part of their own lived identity?
  2. How might sustainability move from being an external issue to an everyday cultural behaviour rooted in place?

The intervention therefore becomes less about delivering information and more about building relationships, between people and place, communities and ecosystems, knowledge and action. A chain reaction of localised actions, shared narratives, educational encounters, and behavioural shifts that slowly reshape how societies relate to nature itself.

I. Towards Indigenous Knowledge Systems

The first intervention emerged from a simple observation: migration often disconnects people from the ecological and cultural histories of the places they inhabit. In cities like Bengaluru, residents arrive carrying memories, habits, and knowledge systems from elsewhere, while remaining unfamiliar with the indigenous ecological intelligence of the land beneath them. Lakes become layouts, native species become background scenery, and local histories disappear into infrastructure.

The intervention therefore asks: What if becoming a resident also meant becoming responsible to the region?

The proposed "Social Card" acts as an entry point into Indigenous Knowledge Systems (IKS). Through collaborations with institutes, companies, and housing societies, newly arriving residents are introduced to the ecological identity of the region within their first weeks of settling into the city.

Rather than functioning as surveillance or bureaucracy, the card becomes a cultural onboarding system. It offers access to regional ecological information, public participation opportunities, local environmental histories, utility incentives, and community engagement networks. The intention is not to force belonging, but to cultivate ecological familiarity. To slowly shift the identity of an urban resident from temporary consumer to regional participant.

II. Towards Bioregions

If ecological disconnection is partly a crisis of memory, then bioregional thinking becomes a way of remembering place again. The first layer begins with visibility.

Public interactions, street media, and contextual storytelling are designed to sensitise residents to the forgotten ecological systems embedded within the city. "There was a lake here a decade ago."

Simple prompts like these interrupt routine urban movement and encourage residents to question the layered histories beneath their surroundings. Lakes, drainage systems, vegetation, and settlement patterns are reframed as living markers of the region's identity rather than invisible infrastructure.

The intervention treats curiosity as the first step toward stewardship. Because before people protect a landscape, they must first learn to see it.

The second layer focuses on participation through schools and youth communities. The proposed "Dhara Tiny Think Tank" introduces students between 4th and 10th grade to regional ecological realities through observation, participation, dialogue, and collective action. Instead of sustainability being treated as abstract information, students become regional advocates capable of influencing conversations within homes, schools, and communities.

The goal is not simply environmental education. It is the cultivation of regional responsibility.

The final system imagines region specific ecological governance rooted in local realities rather than universal sustainability templates. Governments, institutions, researchers, and communities collaboratively define ecological goals based on the conditions of the region itself, its water systems, biodiversity, movement patterns, waste streams, and cultural behaviours.

Bioregionalism here is not imagined as isolation. It is imagined as ecological literacy at the scale of everyday life. A way for people to understand where they are, what sustains them, and how their actions ripple across the systems they inhabit. Together, these smaller interventions build toward the larger bioregional framework.

"We can't control systems or figure them out. But we can dance with them."

— Donella Meadows

Perhaps the future does not ask us to dominate nature more intelligently, but to participate within it more consciously.
To listen before extracting.
To belong before building.
To understand that sustainability is not a fixed solution, but an ongoing relationship with the systems that sustain life.
