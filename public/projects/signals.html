02 — Approach & Framing

A systems lens on
an emerging frontier

This project emerged from firsthand experience scaling biomaterial production at Absolute, India's leading bioscience company — where the gap between laboratory innovation and societal adoption became viscerally clear. Validated by futurist Arto Sivonen (Mandag, Helsinki), the research was anchored in Strategic Design Management methodology at NID Gandhinagar.

The project unpacks a system riddled with nomenclature confusion, greenwashing, scaling barriers, and missing infrastructure — and asks: what design interventions can accelerate India's transition to a bioabled world?

Scope 1: Megatrend analysis of bioscience & bio-based innovation
Scope 2: Challenges in the India bio-based industry system

03 — Methodology

A structured journey from signals to solutions

The research roadmap followed a rigorous dual-track approach — integrating secondary research with deep primary immersion — guided by the principle: "First, what could I do? Second, what could I read? And third, who could I ask?"

01
Secondary Research — Getting the beat
Netnography of the industry nomenclature landscape (bio-inspired, bio-based, bio-mimetic, bio-informed). Megatrend mapping — environmental, social, technological, economic trends shaping bio-based adoption through 2050. Material coordinate systems, market analysis, driver mapping.
02
Primary Research — Expert panels & immersion
Delphi interviews with 8 stakeholders — scientists, architects, entrepreneurs, futurists. Factory visits to Cocotuft (coir manufacturing, Cherthala). Observational study at Terrarium founder event, Bengaluru. Participatory research event "Home Tomorrow" at SEEDKochi.
03
Synthesis — Sum of parts
Systems thinking applied to the full bio-based landscape. PESTEL + Porter's 5 Forces challenge analysis. Calotropis seed visualization of innovator mapping. Insight formulation across regulatory, mindset, technological, environmental, and operational dimensions.
04
Ideation & Solutions
Six hat thinking and prototype generation. Design usability testing. Two complementary solution sets: Signals (micro-scale digital library) and the Circular Materials Collective (macro-scale PPP model). Kombucha leather experiment as proof-of-concept.

04 — Field Research

"Home Tomorrow"
participatory installation

Conducted at SEEDKochi — an architecture college's annual student exhibition — the participatory research brought the future of bio-based materials to life in a domestic, relatable context. 50+ attendees, including students and general public, interacted with real bio-material prototypes.

Step 1
A peek into the future
A curated narration painted the picture of home in 2040 — using megatrends and weak signals to expand participants' imagination about what could be possible.
Step 2
Material literacy quiz
Participants were tested on which common household materials biodegrade — revealing a striking knowledge gap. Clay was the most misidentified as biodegradable.
Step 3
Touch, feel, decide
Physical prototypes of Malai bio-leather were presented. Participants chose between bio-leather and conventional leather at different price points — revealing cost and aesthetics as the primary adoption barriers.
Insight
A new material will only be adopted if its cost and aesthetics match or exceed those of existing options.
The research validated a hypothesis that India was at a nascent 'innovation culture' stage — and that there is significant scope for systemic planning to drive this transition forward.

05 — Key Insights

What the system
revealed about itself

Insight 01

Circularity is ignored in the highlight of "bio"

Founders focus on the upstream story of innovation while overlooking end-of-life. Home composting is not legally approved in India. Industrial composting ≠ consumer behaviour. Reusability is an untapped market.

Insight 02

Nomenclature is a conscious blind eye

Bio-based, bio-mimetic, bio-inspired, bio-informed — conflated terms create greenwashing and consumer confusion. The industry lacks a shared, accessible taxonomy that enables informed decision-making.

Insight 03

Innovation in India runs ahead of its business muscle

Delphi analysis revealed founders are heavily ideation-oriented but weaker on community, collaboration, and scaling. The pattern: innovation → technology → business model → regulation. Business is the bottleneck.

Insight 04

Perception of sophistication locks out adoption

Bio-based materials read as "distant" and "lab-only" to the public. Yet from fermentation to crafts, many processes are deeply familiar. The gap is awareness and proximity — not technological access.

Insight 05

Circularity demands a localized model of innovation

Glocalization — the shift of economic power to the East — creates a unique window for India. Startups must source locally, build domestic demand, and think from the circular lifecycle outward, not the product inward.

Insight 06

Co-opetition is the only way to scale at speed

"When you innovate, you innovate it for you and for the world. But no one bio-based company can serve the entire world." — Anu, Varsya. Open-source knowledge sharing and cooperative infrastructure are essential.

06 — Solution Set

A multifaceted response to the ever-growing bio-material industry

Two complementary solutions operate at different scales — a micro-level digital platform to democratise knowledge, and a macro-level systemic model to physically convene stakeholders in a region.

Solution 01 · Digital Platform

nature Signals

A living library to find, share, and innovate with bio-based, bio-inspired, and bio-mimetic materials. Signals is designed as a dynamic, growing repository — the world's largest of its kind for natural raw materials.

Unlike static archives, Signals integrates End-Of-Life management as a core feature — requiring contributors to explain how their innovation degrades, composts, or re-enters the cycle. This makes circularity non-optional.

  • 80+ raw materials mapped in the Innovation Wheel with primary material, secondary additive, and outcome product layers
  • EOL (End-Of-Life) field required on every submission — shifting conversation from birth to full lifecycle
  • Searchable by material feel, outcome, innovator, industry sector, and phase of innovation
  • Collaboration portal connecting scientists, designers, architects, manufacturers, and researchers
  • Ownership and IP attribution visible — fostering open-source knowledge culture
  • Feedback loop (Reddit-style) enabling authentic community review of innovations
  • Tech partnership: NT Global. Validation: Mandag (Arto Sivonen, Helsinki)
Signals platform prototype — Innovation Wheel
Ongoing experiment — testing the wheel

Kombucha leather

An experiment to test the Signals wheel in action — grown in a home environment over 28 days to form a SCOBY leather, then dried for 3 additional days. The resulting material is flexible with lower tensile strength — opening the door for lemongrass as a secondary additive. Batch 2 is underway.

Tea brew → SCOBY mother culture → 2.5 weeks → drying → initial prototype

Solution 02 · Systemic Model

Circular Materials Collective

A Public-Private Partnership (PPP) model designed for the state of Kerala — and replicable across any Indian state with agricultural biomass. The CMC acts as a catalyst for the bio-based materials industry by physically bringing together manufacturers, startups, scientists, government bodies, institutions, think tanks, architects, designers, and farmers.

The model is grounded in the "co-opetition" philosophy — individual players benefit by sharing knowledge and infrastructure, while competing in the market. Kerala, with 2% of India's bio-tech startups but enormous agricultural biodiversity, is the ideal starting node.

  • Cradle-to-cradle material lifecycle as the ideological foundation
  • Connects Kerala's 6-7 existing bio-material startups with traditional manufacturers (eg. Cocotuft, 100 years old) lacking innovation
  • Banana fibre working scenario: Cocotuft has an international bulk order — CMC matches them with Ar. Anooja J's research on banana pseudostem fibre extraction
  • Three-phase growth: awareness collaboration → material management → full PPP formation
  • Scalable as a hub-and-spoke model across Indian states with high biomass production
  • Aligned with KSUM, KSIDC, KSBB, Life Science Park, Rebuild Kerala Initiative

07 — Kerala in Limelight

A tropical goldmine waiting to be activated

Despite extraordinary biodiversity and agricultural wealth — coconuts, bananas, tapioca, rubber, coir — Kerala accounts for just 2% of India's biotech startup registrations. The research examined why, and what structural design interventions could unlock this potential.

Barrier 01

Land, labour, and rent costs are prohibitive for early-stage startups in Kerala — especially relative to cities like Bangalore, Hyderabad, and Delhi.

Barrier 02

Missing micro-economy infrastructure — labs, chemical suppliers, and equipment — delays prototyping and testing phases that all biomaterial startups depend on.

Barrier 03

Government funds and proceedings (KSUM) are delayed. State schemes are less accessible than central ones. Capacity building for manufacturing tangible goods is absent from the startup support framework.

"Kerala being a tropical paradise has a lot of potential to become a testament to circular city — where all materials are either biodegradable or recyclable."

— Varsha Nambiar, Research Thesis

08 — Outcomes & Impact

What this project set in motion

The project has catalysed a shift in societal thinking about materials and sustainability in Kerala — and produced tangible design artefacts, institutional collaborations, and a roadmap for an emerging circular bioeconomy.
